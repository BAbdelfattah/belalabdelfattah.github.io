The Damaged Cans Dilemma: A Real Food Safety Case from the Field

How should food safety professionals evaluate dented cans, rust, and leakage in real warehouse operations?

In food safety, some situations look small at first… but the decision behind them is anything but simple. Issues like dented cans, rusted cans, or leaking canned food are common in warehouses and distribution centers. However, the decision to accept or reject a shipment is rarely straightforward.

The First Signs

  • Some cans had dents
  • Some cans showed light rust
  • A few cartons were wet or stained with sauce

Evaluation: Category Approach

  • Category A – Critical Defects: Leaking cans, swollen cans, punctures, evidence of spoilage → rejected automatically
  • Category B – Major Defects: Severe dents near seams, advanced rust, deformation → segregated and rejected
  • Category C – Minor Defects: Light scratches, small dents away from seams → may be accepted after proper evaluation

The Real Problem Was Not the Cans

The main issue was logistical handling—pressure during transport, rough handling, and kitting operations caused denting, leakage, and staining.

The Decision

  • Segregate heavily damaged cartons
  • Reject cans with leakage or rust
  • Reinspect and clean affected cartons where possible
  • Accept only minor dents away from seams

Lesson for Food Safety Professionals

Good quality management is about understanding the full picture and making the best professional decision with the information available.

Fruits & Vegetables: Handling Hazards in Real Operations

A Practical Food Safety Case from the Field

How should food safety professionals handle fruits and vegetables when quality problems come from inside the supply chain? This real operational case explores the challenge of receiving deteriorated produce, managing risks, and protecting food safety under operational pressure.

When people think about food safety risks, they often imagine processing plants, complex machinery, or packaged foods. But some of the most underestimated products in food safety are actually the simplest ones: fresh fruits and vegetables. Unlike packaged food, fresh produce often reaches the consumer with minimal processing, and sometimes it is consumed raw. This means that any deterioration, contamination, or mishandling can directly affect food safety.

I remember a situation when I was working in a company that had a central distribution center responsible for supplying branches with food products, including fresh fruits and vegetables. In theory, the system seemed simple. The central warehouse would send fruits and vegetables to the branches, where they would be used for:

  • Product display
  • Fresh juices
  • Fruit salads
  • Kitchen preparation

When Quality Problems Come from Inside the System

Over time we started noticing a recurring issue. Some of the fruits and vegetables arriving from the central distribution center were already in poor condition. Typical problems included:

  • Overripe fruits
  • Soft or bruised vegetables
  • Damaged produce inside crates
  • Early signs of spoilage

The Operational Dilemma

Rejecting poor-quality produce sounds simple in theory. However, in real operations, the situation can be more complicated. These products were supplied internally by the company’s own distribution system, and rejecting shipments was not always operationally easy. This created a daily dilemma: How do you manage food safety risks when the supply chain itself is not performing properly?

Why Fruits and Vegetables Can Become a Food Safety Risk

  • Microbial growth
  • Cross-contamination with other foods
  • Attraction of insects and pests
  • Rapid spoilage of nearby products

When Produce Is Used for Multiple Purposes

  • Fresh juices
  • Fruit salads
  • Kitchen ingredients

Managing the Risk at the Operational Level

  • Immediate sorting of fruits and vegetables after delivery
  • Removing damaged or spoiled produce
  • Separating questionable items from kitchen preparation areas
  • Monitoring products used for juices and salads carefully

The Lesson for Food Safety Professionals

Food safety is not only about rules and standards. Sometimes it is about managing imperfect systems and operational limitations. When the supply chain is not ideal, the responsibility of the food safety professional becomes even more critical. Understanding risks, applying practical controls, and maintaining professional judgment are essential parts of real-world food safety.
